## 1. What do meta-analyses and RCTs show for running economy, VO2max, time-trial performance and sprint/finishing kick?

### Takeaway
Across four meta-analyses and one systematic review (2016-2024), strength training added to running consistently improves running economy (RE). Effects run from small to large, about 2-8% in individual trials. High-load (≥80% 1RM) training also improves time-trial and time-to-exhaustion performance. VO2max, vVO2max, maximal metabolic steady state and sprint capacity change only trivially. Strength training works mainly through economy and neuromuscular factors, not through aerobic power.

### Cited Findings
**Balsalobre-Fernández, Santos-Concejero & Grivas 2016 (J Strength Cond Res; meta-analysis)**
- 5 controlled trials, n = 93 competitive, high-level middle- and long-distance runners. Pooled effect on RE: SMD = -1.42 (95% CI -2.23 to -0.60), i.e. large and beneficial (lower O2 cost) — [PubMed 26694507](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26694507/)
- Programs were modest: 4 of 5 studies used 40-70% 1RM at low-moderate volume, 2-3 sessions/week for 8-12 weeks. Quality limits: only 5 studies and a wide CI — [PubMed 26694507](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26694507/)

**Denadai et al. 2017 (Sports Med 47(3):545-554; meta-analysis)**
- Concurrent strength training had a small beneficial effect on RE. Explosive and heavy-weight programs improved RE about equally. Isometric training produced no significant effect — [UNESP repository](https://repositorio.unesp.br/handle/11449/39879?show=full); [SPONET record](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4015241?lng=en)
- Meta-regression: the % improvement in RE was larger with longer training duration. Explosive or heavy training improves RE "within a few weeks", but long-term programs are needed for the largest gains — [UNESP repository](https://repositorio.unesp.br/handle/11449/39879?show=full)
- Related primary RCT (Guglielmo, Greco & Denadai 2009; 4 weeks, n = 16 runners): RE improved only in the heavy-weight group, not in the explosive group trained on the same equipment — [Thieme/IJSM](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/s-2008-1038792.pdf)

**Blagrove, Howatson & Hayes 2018 (Sports Med; systematic review, no meta-analysis)**
- 24 studies met the inclusion criteria, with PEDro scores of 4-6 (moderate internal validity). RE was measured in 20 studies and generally improved by 2-8% versus control, though not in every study — [PMC5889786](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5889786/); [Loughborough repository](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Effects_of_strength_training_on_the_physiological_determinants_of_middle-_and_long-distance_running_performance_a_systematic_review/9619553)
- Summary from the same body of literature: heavy or explosive strength training has a small positive effect on RE, laboratory performance measures and 3-10 km time trials, but does not improve VO2max or lactate threshold — [LIDA record of a related review](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4092479?lng=en) (secondary summary)

**Eihara et al. 2022 (meta-analysis comparing heavy resistance training with plyometrics)**
- Pooled RE effect: heavy resistance training g = -0.32, plyometric training g = -0.13 (negative means better economy) — cited in [Sports Medicine Open 2022 PDF](https://sportsmedicine-open.springeropen.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s40798-022-00511-1) (secondary citation; original not retrieved)

**Llanos-Lagos, Ramirez-Campillo, Moran & Sáez de Villarreal 2024a (Sports Med 54(4):895-932; RE at different speeds)**
- Three-level random-effects meta-analysis with moderator analysis. Databases searched up to November 2022. Participants: 195 moderately trained, 272 well-trained and 185 highly trained runners — [UNAB repository](https://repositorio.unab.cl/items/13e3ac75-9905-4902-81f7-232752e84ae6/full); [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/37272/)
- High-load training, plyometrics and combined methods improve RE compared with control. Submaximal-load and isometric training appear less effective — [UNAB repository](https://repositorio.unab.cl/items/13e3ac75-9905-4902-81f7-232752e84ae6/full)
- High-load training improved RE at speeds from 8.64 to 17.85 km/h. Plyometric training improved RE only at speeds ≤12.00 km/h. Moderators: the high-load effect was larger at higher speeds and in runners with higher VO2max — [UNAB repository](https://repositorio.unab.cl/items/13e3ac75-9905-4902-81f7-232752e84ae6/full)
- Certainty of evidence (GRADE): moderate for high-load, submaximal, plyometric and isometric training; low for combined methods — [UNAB repository](https://repositorio.unab.cl/items/13e3ac75-9905-4902-81f7-232752e84ae6/full)

**Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024b (Sports Med; DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02018-z; athletic performance)**
- 38 studies, n = 894 middle- and long-distance runners. Certainty of evidence: very low to moderate — [PMC11258194](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11258194/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02018-z)
- High-load training (≥80% 1RM) can improve time-trial and time-to-exhaustion performance. Combining two or more strength methods may give larger gains than a single method — [PMC11258194](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11258194/)
- VO2max, vVO2max, maximal metabolic steady state and sprint capacity showed trivial changes after strength training — [PMC11258194](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11258194/)

**Plyometric-specific meta-analysis (Kinesiology, 2023; 18 studies reviewed, 10 meta-analysed)**
- Plyometric jump training alone had a trivial-to-small effect on RE. Combined with resistance training, the effect was large. Larger gains came with >15 total sessions, >7 weeks and >2 days/week, and in younger, more highly trained runners — [UNAB repository](https://repositorio.unab.cl/handle/ria/55283); [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4086380?lng=en)

### Inferences
- Taken together: RE is the main transfer route. Expect roughly 2-8% better RE and small-to-moderate time-trial gains. Expect no meaningful change in VO2max, so strength training complements aerobic training and does not replace it.
- The point estimates shrink as the evidence base grows and methods improve: Balsalobre 2016 SMD -1.42 with 5 studies, versus Eihara 2022 g ≈ -0.13 to -0.32. The modest values are the more realistic expectation.
- On sprint/finishing kick, the pooled evidence (Llanos-Lagos 2024b) shows trivial changes, so the claim that strength training improves the finishing kick is not supported at meta-analytic level.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled SMDs with CIs for each method and outcome in Llanos-Lagos 2024a/b could not be extracted because the full texts were unreachable.
- Blagrove 2018's specific time-trial and anaerobic/sprint findings could not be verified from the full text.
- Eihara 2022 was not retrieved directly. Its g values come from a secondary citation.
- All meta-analyses cover flat-running RE (treadmill, mostly 0-1% grade) and track or road time trials. None pools trail or uphill outcomes.

## 2. Which types of training work best (heavy >80% 1RM, plyometric, explosive, combined), in well-trained versus recreational runners?

### Takeaway
High-load (≥80% 1RM) training has the most consistent support for both RE and time-trial performance, and its benefit grows at faster speeds and in fitter runners. Plyometrics help mainly at slower speeds (≤12 km/h) and work best combined with heavy training. Combined programs may be best overall, but with lower certainty. Isometric and submaximal-load training are least effective.

### Cited Findings
- High load, plyometric and combined training improve RE. Submaximal and isometric training are less effective. The high-load benefit is larger at higher speeds and higher VO2max — [Llanos-Lagos 2024a, UNAB](https://repositorio.unab.cl/items/13e3ac75-9905-4902-81f7-232752e84ae6/full)
- Plyometric RE gains were seen only at ≤12 km/h, which is the range of many trail and ultra paces — [Llanos-Lagos 2024a, UNAB](https://repositorio.unab.cl/items/13e3ac75-9905-4902-81f7-232752e84ae6/full)
- ≥80% 1RM training improves time-trial and time-to-exhaustion performance. Combining methods may beat a single method — [Llanos-Lagos 2024b, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11258194/)
- Explosive and heavy training gave similar RE improvements, while isometric training gave none — [Denadai 2017, UNESP](https://repositorio.unesp.br/handle/11449/39879?show=full)
- Heavy resistance training: g = -0.32. Plyometrics: g = -0.13 — [Eihara 2022 as cited in Sports Med Open](https://sportsmedicine-open.springeropen.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s40798-022-00511-1)
- Plyometrics alone have a small effect on RE, but combined with resistance training the effect is large. Benefits are greater in more highly trained runners and with >7 weeks, >2 sessions/week — [Kinesiology 2023 meta-analysis, UNAB](https://repositorio.unab.cl/handle/ria/55283)
- Even 40-70% 1RM programs gave a large RE effect in high-level runners, though this rests on only 5 studies — [Balsalobre-Fernández 2016, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26694507/)
- Ultra-endurance athletes: 12 weeks of a home-based strength + explosive + plyometric (SEP) program in well-trained male ultra-marathoners (n = 25; 13 SEP, 12 control) reduced the energy cost of running by ~3-6% at all tested speeds, with no change in controls. Changes in energy cost were inversely related to gains in lower-limb maximal power, and ground contact time increased. The authors recommend at least 3 SEP sessions/week alongside endurance training — Giovanelli et al. 2017, Eur J Sport Sci — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4043269?lng=en); [Giovanelli PhD thesis, Univ. Udine](https://air.uniud.it/bitstream/11390/1132913/1/10990_676_PhD%20thesis%20final_Giovanelli_print.pdf)
- A secondary summary puts the average gain at ~4.3% and projects that a 4% lower energy cost could save ~17 min over 100 km. This is a model projection, not a measured race result — [Fisiologia del Ejercicio summary](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/efectos-del-entrenamiento-fuerza-explosivo-pliometrico-gasto-energetico-carrera-atletas-ultraresistencia/)
- Suggested heavy/explosive dose from a 2024 review: 2-3 sets per exercise at ~75-100% 1RM, twice weekly in pre-season — reported in search summary of [MDPI review](https://www.mdpi.com/2920186) (secondary; full text not verified)

### Inferences
- For trail and ultra runners, who often run at ≤12 km/h on flat-equivalent terrain, plyometrics may transfer relatively better than at fast road paces. Heavy loading remains the most robust choice. A combined heavy + plyometric approach is the best-supported prescription.
- Recreational versus well-trained runners: the moderator analyses suggest larger high-load effects in fitter runners and at higher speeds. This does not mean recreational runners do not benefit; the comparison is mostly within trained samples.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis reports moderators by sex. I found no sex-stratified analysis.
- The 2025 Journal of Medical Science and Sports Health meta-analysis on plyometrics in recreational runners was found but its results could not be retrieved — [UNY journal](https://journal.uny.ac.id/publications/jomassh/article/download/1200/570/6922)
- No head-to-head RCT compared heavy and plyometric training in trail runners on trail-specific outcomes.

## 3. Evidence specific to uphill and downhill running, eccentric loading, neuromuscular fatigue in ultra/trail races, and resistance to muscle damage

### Takeaway
Direct RCTs of structured strength training with trail outcomes are scarce. The best evidence is one 2026 three-arm RCT in masters trail runners, where circuit weight training alone did not improve an 18 km mountain time trial but uphill HIIT and combined training did. Correlational work shows maximal leg-extensor strength predicts both uphill and downhill performance. Downhill/eccentric work causes large force loss and muscle damage, and repeated eccentric/downhill exposure appears protective. Mountain ultras cause about 24-39% MVC losses in knee extensors and plantar flexors.

### Cited Findings
**Trail RCT**
- Panthong, Tongsiri, Tanaka & Suksom, J Strength Cond Res 2026 (DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000005622). Forty masters trail runners were randomized to 12 weeks of circuit weight training, uphill HIIT, or both, each alongside 3 endurance sessions + 3 modality-specific sessions/week — [Marathon Handbook summary](https://marathonhandbook.com/the-best-training-mix-for-masters-trail-runners/); [MTI summary](https://mtntactical.com/?p=1478255)
  - 18 km mountain-trail time trial: improved in the HIIT and combined groups, not significantly in circuit-only. Uphill RE improved most with uphill HIIT — [Marathon Handbook](https://marathonhandbook.com/the-best-training-mix-for-masters-trail-runners/)
  - Circuit and combined groups increased lean mass, strength, jump power and MVC. All groups improved VO2max, anaerobic fitness, leg stiffness and dynamic balance — [MTI summary](https://mtntactical.com/?p=1478255)
  - Enrolment discrepancy: one summary reports 45 enrolled and 5 withdrawals for injury/illness — [Marathon Handbook](https://marathonhandbook.com/the-best-training-mix-for-masters-trail-runners/). An earlier version of the study is a 2023 Chulalongkorn dissertation with the same design — [Chula ETD](https://digital.car.chula.ac.th/chulaetd/10506)
  - Quality caveat: circuit weight training is not heavy (≥80% 1RM) strength training, so the null result for circuit-only does not test heavy strength protocols.

**Determinants of uphill and downhill performance**
- Lemire et al. 2021 (J Sci Med Sport). Ten highly trained male endurance athletes ran 5 km uphill and downhill time trials at ±8% grade. Uphill performance was predicted by vVO2max + BMI + maximal extensor strength (r² = 0.94). Downhill performance was predicted by vVO2max + leg musculotendinous stiffness + maximal extensor strength (r² = 0.84) — [Unistra lab page via search](https://lab3072.unistra.fr/publications/publications-marquantes/physiological-factors-determining-downhill-vs-uphill-running-endurance-performance)
- Lemire et al. 2021 (Front Physiol). The energy cost of running was inversely correlated with knee-extensor isometric maximal torque on level and downhill slopes, but not uphill — [Frontiers in Physiology 10.3389/fphys.2021.697315](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.697315/pdf)
- A small randomized study (n = 14 active volunteers) of uphill versus downhill intervals at ±10% grade found significant changes only in the downhill group, e.g. squat jump +9.5 ± 11.7% (p < .05). The sample is very small — [search summary; source record](https://producciocientifica.uv.es/documentos/6a94575d567b2f9793113425)
- High-intensity downhill running increased heart rate and muscular fatigue in trail runners (Strasbourg group) — [Unistra publication listing](https://lab3072.unistra.fr/publications/publications-marquantes/high-intensity-downhill-running-exacerbates-heart-rate-and-muscular-fatigue-in-trail-runners)

**Downhill, eccentric loading and muscle damage**
- Varesco, Coratella, Vernillo et al. 2022 (Eur J Appl Physiol). Downhill running impaired maximal force and rapid force development (RFD). The rapid-force impairment was located in the muscle (possibly lower muscle-tendon stiffness), not in neural drive — [Springer EJAP 10.1007/s00421-022-04990-8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-022-04990-8); [AIR Unimi](https://air.unimi.it/handle/2434/933186)
- Coratella et al. 2024 (EJAP follow-up). Downhill running increased muscle-damage markers and impaired maximal voluntary force. The authors propose late-phase RFD as an additional muscle-damage marker in trail running — [Springer EJAP 10.1007/s00421-023-05412-z](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00421-023-05412-z)
- Martinez-Navarro et al. 2026 (exploratory study). Thirty-six trail runners were tested before a 106 km ultra. A 5 km downhill bout at -15% raised all muscle-damage biomarkers. Regular downhill intervals and a more "terrestrial" gait appeared to soften strength loss and the damage response. The authors say confirmation is needed — [PMC12846201](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12846201/); [PDF](https://www.penyagolosatrails.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/21-Downhill-Running-Induced-Muscle-Damage-in-Trail-Runners.pdf)
- A 2018 Frontiers study used trail runners already habituated to regular eccentric training to test compression garments during downhill running. Repeated eccentric exposure is generally accepted to reduce exercise-induced muscle damage (EIMD) — [Frontiers in Physiology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.01627/pdf)
- Isometric pre-conditioning RCT (2018; n = 30 untrained men). Maximal isometric leg-press contractions 2 days before 30 min of downhill running at -15% reduced soreness at 48 h and sped strength recovery, but did not attenuate the post-downhill change in RE — [UFC repository](https://repositorio.ufc.br/handle/riufc/62106?locale=en)
- A single downhill bout attenuated fatigue from later level running (Sci Rep 2020) — reported in search summary; primary not retrieved
- Downhill running emphasizes eccentric work of the knee extensors, tibial muscles and hip extensors (1995 review) — [BJSM via PMC1332286](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1332286/)

**Neuromuscular fatigue in mountain ultras**
- Millet et al. 2011 (PLoS ONE): 166 km mountain ultra with ~9,500 m positive and negative elevation change. Large MVC losses: knee extensors ≈ -35%, plantar flexors ≈ -39% (signs garbled in the abstract extraction; verify). Function returned to baseline by 16 days, mostly within 9 days — [PMC3043077](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3043077/); [HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01659915)
- 110 km race: MVC loss ≈ 35% knee extensors and ≈ 28% plantar flexors. 330 km Tor des Géants: smaller losses (≈ -24% knee extensors, -26% plantar flexors), with no further MVC decline after ~30-36 h of running. This suggests pacing and protective strategies in longer races, and large central fatigue — [PLoS ONE 2013](https://www.plosone.org/article/info%3Adoi%2F10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0065596); [Northumbria](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/effect-of-the-fatigue-induced-by-a-110-km-ultramarathon-on-tibial/)
- After a 110 km mountain ultra, women showed less peripheral plantar-flexor fatigue than men (2020 conference abstract) — [HAL](https://hal.uca.fr/hal-02996198)
- Non-randomized pre-post study of 12 ultratrail runners after a strength program: better force production and core stability, lower perceptual fatigue indicators. No control group; publication status unclear (2026) — [UPSE repository](https://repositorio.upse.edu.ec/bitstreams/6b73670a-3c9c-4579-9992-e82948c64c51/download)

**Practitioner synthesis**
- A Spanish summary document on mountain running and strength (Mayayo / Balsalobre) — [carrerasdemontana.com PDF](https://carrerasdemontana.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/carreras-de-montana-y-entrenamiento-fuerza-estudios-mayayo.-carlos-basalobre.pdf) (not read; content unverified)

### Inferences
- Because maximal extensor strength predicts both uphill and downhill performance, and knee-extensor strength relates to lower energy cost on level and downhill slopes, heavy lower-body strength is plausibly most valuable for downhill and late-race resistance. Uphill performance seems driven more by aerobic capacity and specific uphill training.
- Downhill damage is mostly peripheral (muscle-level RFD loss), and repeated eccentric exposure protects. So eccentric-biased strength work plus regular downhill running is a logical, mechanism-based strategy, but RCT proof of better race performance is lacking.
- The circuit-only null in Panthong 2026 is a reminder that low-intensity circuit work is not a substitute for specific uphill training. Combined training gave both the trail-time benefit and strength gains.

### Gaps
- No RCT found that tests heavy (≥80% 1RM) or eccentric strength training on downhill-running performance or muscle damage in trail runners.
- No RCT found linking a strength program to measured ultra-trail race performance or to in-race neuromuscular fatigue (MVC loss).
- No published Vernillo/Millet intervention trial found on strength training; their work is mainly observational fatigue research.
- Full numbers for the Giovanelli 2017 trial (e.g., power changes, speeds tested) and for Panthong 2026 (effect sizes, time-trial deltas) could not be retrieved.

## 4. Does strength training cause weight gain or an interference effect in endurance runners?

### Takeaway
Within typical 6-14 week concurrent programs, the evidence shows no meaningful gain in body mass and no harm to VO2max or endurance performance in runners. The interference effect is real, but it mostly blunts strength and hypertrophy gains (especially in the lower body and at high endurance volumes), not running performance.

### Cited Findings
- Strength training does not reduce VO2max; pooled VO2max changes after strength training are trivial — [Llanos-Lagos 2024b, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11258194/)
- A 2024 review reports that heavy and explosive strength training improved RE with no change in VO2max, and judges the concern that added muscle harms endurance to be unwarranted — reported in search summary of [MDPI review](https://www.mdpi.com/2920186) (secondary)
- Concurrent training can raise maximal strength with little hypertrophy. Fear of gaining mass is a common reason runners avoid strength work — [Concurrent training in elite male runners (PDF)](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Concurrent%20Training%20in%20Elite%20Male%20Runners%20-%20The%20Influence%20of%20Strength%20Versus%20Muscular%20Endurance%20Training%20on%20Performance%20Outcomes.pdf)
- Interference: higher endurance volumes impair strength and hypertrophy more; the effect is local to muscles used in the endurance exercise and mostly in the lower body. Block periodization can reduce it. Strength training often adds benefit, but interference depends on dose and other factors — [Review: interference or additive effects (SPONET)](https://sponet.de/Record/4060491); [LIDA](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4060491)
- A 12-week study of recreational runners found no significant change in body mass, partly attributed to the short duration — reported in search summary ([PMC5407958](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5407958)) (secondary)
- In the masters trail RCT, circuit and combined training increased lean mass without hurting trail time-trial gains in the combined group — [MTI summary of Panthong 2026](https://mtntactical.com/?p=1478255)

### Inferences
- For trail and ultra runners, a small lean-mass increase is unlikely to cost uphill performance (BMI is a predictor of uphill performance in Lemire 2021) as long as total body mass is stable. Heavy, low-rep, low-volume work emphasizes neural adaptations over hypertrophy.
- To limit interference: keep strength sessions low in volume, separate them from key running sessions, and periodize (block approach).

### Gaps
- No long-term (>6 months) data on body mass in runners doing heavy strength training.
- No trail-specific data on whether lean-mass gains change uphill energy cost.
- Classic references (e.g., Hickson 1980; Wilson 2012 meta-analysis; Beattie et al. 2017 on body mass in distance runners; Rønnestad & Mujika 2014) are relevant but their figures were not verified in this session.
