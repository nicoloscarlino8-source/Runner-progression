# Programming strength training for trail and ultra-trail runners

> Method note: full-text fetching (PubMed Central, Uphill Athlete, etc.) was blocked by the network proxy in this session, so the findings below come from search-engine extracts of abstracts, repositories, coach pages and podcasts. Numbers attributed to primary papers should be checked against the full text before they are quoted in a final report. Each point is labelled **[EVIDENCE]** (peer-reviewed study, review or meta-analysis) or **[PRACTICE]** (coach, practitioner or commercial source).

## 1. Frequency, intensity, volume, and the minimal effective dose for in-season maintenance

### Takeaway
Research supports **2–3 sessions a week for 8–12+ weeks** in a development phase, mixing heavy resistance work (≥80% 1RM, ≤8 reps; the classic protocol is 4×4RM squats) with plyometrics or explosive work. In season, **about 1 session a week at high intensity** (low volume, kept heavy) appears to maintain the gains. The athlete-specific data behind that maintenance figure are thin, and much of it comes from soccer, cycling and general populations.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE]** Blagrove, Howatson & Hayes (2018, *Sports Medicine* 48:1117–1149), a systematic review of 24 studies (PEDro 4–6) in runners with at least 6 months of experience: running economy improved by **2–8%** compared with running-only controls in most of the 20 studies that measured it. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-017-0835-7); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29249083/)
- **[EVIDENCE]** The same review concludes that "the addition of **two to three ST sessions per week**, which include a variety of ST modalities, are likely to provide benefits" to middle- and long-distance runners. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321859631_Effects_of_Strength_Training_on_the_Physiological_Determinants_of_Middle-_and_Long-Distance_Running_Performance_A_Systematic_Review)
- **[PRACTICE / expert]** Blagrove's teaching slides (2025) define heavy resistance training as **≤8 reps per set at ≥80% 1RM**, explosive training as **30–79% 1RM**, and list plyometrics as a separate modality. The slides also mention organising the week through "micro-dosing". — [Blagrove slides, Mar 2025](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/57f8526ce3df285aab609e84/t/67cac1c36189bc149514b897/1741341125503/Strength+for+Endurance+-+Principles+of+S&C+Mar25+-+Rich+Blagrove.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE]** A 2016 meta-analysis in highly trained middle- and long-distance runners concluded that a programme of "low to high intensity resistance exercises and plyometric exercises performed **2–3 times per week for 8–12 weeks**" is an appropriate strategy for improving running economy. — [PubMed 26694507](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26694507/)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Støren et al. (2008; Helgerud and Hoff co-authors) used **4×4RM half squats, 3×/week for 8 weeks** in distance runners. Running economy improved by 4.6% and time to exhaustion at maximal aerobic speed rose 21.3%. I could not confirm the commonly cited "85% 1RM" from these extracts. — [Paulo Gentil PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Maximal%20Strength%20Training%20Improves%20Running%20Economy%20in%20Distance%20Runners.pdf); [Myworkout summary](https://www.myworkout.com/en/blog/maximal-strength-training-improves-running-economy-in-distance-runners)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Hoff's group found the same maximal-strength model improved work economy in cross-country skiers. — [Coachsci SDSU](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol85/hoff1.htm)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Beattie et al. (2017, *JSCR* 31:9–23) ran a 40-week trial in 20 competitive distance runners: 20 weeks of preseason training at **2 sessions/week** (maximal and reactive strength), then 20 weeks in season at **1 session/week**. Maximal and reactive strength, running economy and vVO2max improved at both week 20 and week 40, with **no change in lean or fat mass**. The 1-a-week in-season dose held, and further increased, the gains. The frequencies come from a secondary summary. — [UL repository](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/the-effect-of-strength-training-on-performance-indicators-in-dist/); [LER summary](https://lermagazine.com/?p=26882)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Spiering et al. (2021, *JSCR*): cutting frequency from 2–3 sessions/week to **1/week** was enough to maintain, or even increase, strength **as long as intensity (and volume per session) was maintained**. "Exercise intensity seems to be the key variable." The authors note that the data come mostly from young adults and that there is not enough data to make specific recommendations for athletes. — [JSCR](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2021/05000/maintaining_physical_performance__the_minimal_dose.35.aspx); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/349637321_Maintaining_Physical_Performance_The_Minimal_Dose_of_Exercise_Needed_to_Preserve_Endurance_and_Strength_Over_Time)
- **[EVIDENCE]** In professional soccer players (Rønnestad et al.), **1 maintenance session/week** over the first 12 weeks of the season preserved the strength, sprint and jump gains from a 10-week preparatory block. Rønnestad's endurance review states that "approximately one strength training session per week (low volume) with high intensity seems to maintain" the adaptations. — [ResearchGate (soccer)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51601921_Effects_of_In-Season_Strength_Maintenance_Training_Frequency_in_Professional_Soccer_Players); [Rønnestad & Mujika 2014, SJMSS](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/sms.12104)
- **[EVIDENCE]** A high volume of endurance training impaired strength adaptations over 12 weeks in well-trained endurance athletes (Rønnestad's group). This matters for ultra runners carrying heavy weekly volume. — [Rønnestad PDF](https://www.euskadi.eus/contenidos/informacion/kirol_eskola_prestakuntza_jard/eu_def/adjuntos/R%C3%B8nnestad.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Giovanelli et al. (2017, *Eur J Sport Sci* 17:805–813) tested a 12-week home programme of strength, explosive and plyometric work in 25 male ultra runners. Running economy improved by **6.5% at 8 km/h, 5.3% at 10, 5.4% at 12 and 4.5% at 14 km/h**, with no change in controls. Secondary sources disagree on the schedule: "3×/week" in one, "~30 min every other day, skipping days after very long or intense runs" in another. — [Rehasport summary](https://www.rehasport.pl/en/training/strength-plyometrics-and-dynamics-reduce-the-energy-cost-of-running,5755,n,4725); [Sports Performance Bulletin](https://sportsperformancebulletin.com/training/strength-conditioning--flexibility/distance-running-why-strength-matters)
- **[EVIDENCE]** In recreational runners, maximal or explosive strength training combined with endurance training improved neuromuscular performance and running economy more than circuit training (2010). — [Thieme, IJSM 2010](https://thieme-connect.de/products/all/doi/10.1055/s-0029-1243639)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Lauersen et al. (2014, *BJSM*), a meta-analysis of 25 trials: strength training cut sports injuries to **less than one third** (RR 0.315), and overuse injuries "could be almost halved". The overuse RR of 0.527 is for all physical-activity programmes, not strength training alone. A 2018 update limited to strength-training RCTs gave RR 0.338 across 6 studies. These are sports injuries in general, not runner-specific. — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/11/871); [HVL open PDF](https://hvlopen.brage.unit.no/hvlopen-xmlui/bitstream/handle/11250/2585394/lauersen.pdf?sequence=4)
- **[PRACTICE]** Structured TrainingPeaks ultra plans use **2 sessions in build weeks and 1 in recovery weeks**, progressing from stability to strength to power. — [TrainingPeaks plan](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/training-plans/running/ultra/tp-211801/trail-ultra-marathon-50-mile-to-100km-18-week-training-plan-with-strength-and-co)
- **[PRACTICE]** David Roche (coach and Leadville 100 record holder) does 1–2 strength sessions/week with simple lifts and calf raises. He avoids heavy split squats and deadlifts because they left him sore for weeks, and his athletes use a 10-minute "Ultra Legs" routine. — [Scientific Triathlon TTS444](https://scientifictriathlon.com/tts444/); [Trail Runner Mag – Ultra Legs](https://www.trailrunnermag.com/training/trail-tips-training/ultra-legs-a-strength-routine-for-ultrarunners/)

### Inferences
- A practical evidence-based prescription: **development** phase at 2 (to 3) sessions/week, heavy multi-joint lifts at about 3–5 sets × 3–6 reps, ≥80–85% 1RM, stopping far from failure (no RIR data was found, so the commonly used RIR 2–3 is coaching practice), plus a low-to-moderate volume of plyometrics. **Maintenance** in season at 1 session/week, keeping the load heavy and cutting sets.
- Gains in running economy come without measurable hypertrophy (Beattie), which answers the common "I'll get heavier" worry among trail runners.
- For ultra runners with very high volume, total strength-training volume should be kept low and intensity high to limit interference, in line with Rønnestad.

### Gaps
- No study was found that sets RIR or velocity-based autoregulation for runners. The NSCA (Barrie 2020) article was not accessible.
- There is no trial of trail or ultra runners comparing 1 vs 2 sessions/week in season. Maintenance figures are extrapolated from road runners (Beattie), soccer and the general population.
- Koop's prescriptions in *Training Essentials for Ultrarunning* (2nd ed.) were not available in the extracts. The search only confirmed that the book has a strength chapter ([CTS](https://trainright.com/ultrarunning/)).

## 2. Most specific exercises for trail running

### Takeaway
The evidence supports **combining** heavy resistance work and plyometrics. Plyometrics alone have a trivial effect on running economy, while the combination has a large one. Trail-specific exercise choice (single-leg work, step-ups, eccentric quad work, soleus, downhill work) rests mostly on biomechanical reasoning and coaching practice. The strongest trail-specific evidence is for downhill running itself, through the repeated-bout effect, rather than for gym exercises.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE]** Dudagoitia Barrio, Ramirez-Campillo et al. (2023, *Kinesiology*), meta-analysis (18 studies in the review, 10 meta-analysed): **plyometrics alone had a trivial effect** on running economy (ES 0.19), while **plyometrics combined with resistance training had a large effect** (ES 1.34). Larger effects came with **>15 total sessions (ES 1.00), >7 weeks (0.95), >2 sessions/week (0.89)**, and in younger (0.95) and highly trained runners (0.94). — [Kinesiology OJS](https://ojs.srce.hr/kinesiology/article/view/24698)
- **[PRACTICE / expert]** Blagrove recommends these patterns: **squats, hip hinges, stepping movements, lunge variations, and calf/Achilles loading**. His session order is a **10 min warm-up, then 10–20 min of plyometrics, then 20 min of resistance training, then 10 min of injury-prevention conditioning**. — [Scientific Triathlon TTS292](https://scientifictriathlon.com/tts292/); [Matthew Boyd Physio](https://matthewboydphysio.com/what-are-the-best-strength-exercises-for-runners/)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Trail demands are distinctive. In a 156 km ultra, knee-extensor strength fell about **41%** by the finish and had only partly recovered after 12 h, while handgrip barely changed, so the fatigue is localised to the legs. Elite trail runners show higher torque and power than road runners. Predictors change with distance: aerobic capacity matters more for shorter races, while baseline strength and body composition matter more around 100 km. — [Frontiers 2025, fspor.2025.1734785](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1734785/full)
- **[EVIDENCE]** A systematic review of muscle damage in trail running (15 studies, 247 participants) found large post-race rises in damage markers and significant drops in neuromuscular performance. — [PMC12921809](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12921809/)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Injury rates in trail runners are **10.7–19.6 injuries per 1,000 h**, compared with 2.5–5.8 in road runners, mostly at the knee, shin and foot. — [Frontiers Bioeng. 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2025.1690023/full)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Downhill running produces a **repeated-bout effect**: a prior downhill bout reduces muscle damage and strength loss in later ones. The protection can last up to about 6 weeks and fades over time, so the authors recommend at least one downhill session in the weeks before a race. — [PMC7606541](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7606541/); [PMC11209549 (trained female runners)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11209549/)
- **[EVIDENCE]** A 2026 exploratory study of 36 experienced trail runners found that those who regularly did downhill repetitions had lower CK after a 5 km downhill bout and better retention of squat strength. The authors note that strength training, especially **isometric work at long muscle lengths**, has shown a similar protective effect in untrained people, but **"no studies have examined this effect in experienced trail runners."** — [PMC12846201](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12846201/)
- **[PRACTICE]** A popular trail-running article reports that in a 1985 study a 3-week gap between downhill sessions still gave protection, which was gone at 9 weeks. Some coaches suggest a downhill "dose" every 2–3 weeks. — [Trail Runner Mag](https://www.trailrunnermag.com/training/trail-tips-training/the-science-of-downhill-running/); [HerTrails](https://www.hertrails.com/blog/train-the-downhill-eccentric-strength-and-the-art-of-coming-down)
- **[PRACTICE]** Uphill Athlete's **muscular endurance (ME)** session: box step-ups (box about 75% of knee height; 6 sets × 10 reps per leg with minimal push from the trailing leg) and front lunges, plus kettlebell swing circuits early on. It starts at bodyweight, and load rises in steps of **+10–20% of bodyweight**. The main progression variable is **shortening rest between sets**. Low-angle trails and flat rucking are judged not to provide enough muscular stress. — [UA ME Workout PDF](https://uphillathlete.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/ME-Uphill-Athlete-Workout-.pdf); [UA: Muscular endurance](https://uphillathlete.com/strength-training/muscular-endurance-for-mountain-athletes/)
- **[PRACTICE]** Trail Runner Magazine suggests **plyometric step-ups 6–8 weeks before the race**: 5–6 sets × 3–5 reps per leg, 2–3 min rest. — [Trail Runner Mag – step-ups](https://www.trailrunnermag.com/training/workouts-training/improve-your-uphill-running-with-step-ups/)
- **[PRACTICE]** Soleus: training with the **knee bent to about 90°** (seated calf raises, bent-knee isometrics) biases the soleus. This rationale is widely repeated, but the sources found are practitioner opinion. — [SimpliFaster](https://simplifaster.com/articles/the-forgotten-plantar-flexor-training-the-soleus/); [Recover Athletics](https://recoverathletics.com/soleus-strengthening-exercises-for-runners/)
- **[PRACTICE]** Quads: lateral or forward step-downs at **3×6–12 per leg** with load as tolerated, and slow eccentrics (about 6 s) in physio progressions. — [E3 Rehab](https://e3rehab.com/strength-training-for-runners/); [Codling Physio](https://www.codling-physio.co.uk/blog/strength-training-plan)
- **[PRACTICE]** Ultra coaching guides emphasise three qualities: **power for climbing, eccentric strength for descending, and single-leg stability** for technical terrain. Single-leg exercises are preferred over bilateral ones. — [RunSpirited](https://www.runspirited.com/single-post/strength-training-guide-for-ultra-runners-building-the-engine-for-the-long-haul)
- **[PRACTICE]** Some trail plans include **upper-body work as well, because trail runners use poles**. — [TrainingPeaks trail plan](https://trainingpeaks.com/training-plans/running/trail/tp-517315/strength-training-for-trailrunners)

### Inferences
- Suggested hierarchy for trail: (1) a heavy base lift (back or front squat, trap-bar deadlift or hip hinge) plus heavy unilateral work (split squat, Bulgarian split squat, weighted step-up); (2) calf and soleus work (heavy straight-knee and bent-knee raises, isometrics); (3) eccentric quad and step-down work plus actual downhill running for the repeated-bout effect; (4) low-to-moderate volume plyometrics (pogo hops, bounding, depth jumps for advanced athletes; plyo step-ups); (5) core and hip (glute med, hamstrings). Nordic hamstring curls are plausible for hamstring injury prevention, but **no runner- or trail-specific evidence was found in this research**.
- Downhill running is probably the "most specific eccentric exercise" and should be planned as a training stimulus, not left to chance.

### Gaps
- No RCT was found comparing gym eccentric work (e.g. slow eccentric squats or step-downs) with downhill running for protection against descent-induced muscle damage in trail runners.
- No specific evidence was found for Nordic curls or core work in trail runners.
- No plyometric dose expressed as foot contacts per session was available in the extracts.

## 3. Periodization from general preparation to the race-specific phase and taper; when to stop heavy and eccentric work

### Takeaway
Both the literature and coaches follow a sequence from **general and maximal strength first** to **specific work (muscular endurance, power) later**, then a maintenance phase. The best-established taper evidence is for endurance volume (about 8–14 days, −41–60% volume with intensity unchanged). For strength work, coaches commonly drop heavy or novel loads about **2–3 weeks before**, keeping light maintenance. **No study gives an exact cutoff**, and protection from downhill exposure lasts up to about 6 weeks.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE/review]** A 2015 *Strength and Conditioning Journal* review on strength training for endurance athletes recommends starting athletes with little strength-training experience on **maximal, high-force, low-velocity** work, then a sequence for advanced athletes of **strength-endurance → basic strength → strength → power**. — [ETSU (NSCA SCJ)](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works/3781)
- **[EVIDENCE]** In a 12-week block-periodized (ATR) study in recreational runners, running-specific strength work improved countermovement jump (CMJ), 1RM squat and running economy. — [PubMed 36078489](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36078489/)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Beattie 2017 used **20 weeks preseason at 2×/week, then 20 weeks in season at 1×/week**, with gains maintained or improved. — [UL](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/the-effect-of-strength-training-on-performance-indicators-in-dist/)
- **[PRACTICE]** Uphill Athlete "always" starts with a **max strength phase of about 4–6 weeks** before moving to **muscular endurance**. — [Uphill Athlete – Trail running strength](https://uphillathlete.com/?p=79071). In the forum, Steve House describes general strength at **2–8 weeks** and max strength and ME at **up to 8 weeks each or until a plateau** (secondary source). — [UA forum](https://uphillathlete.com/forums/topic/tftna-vs-tftua-strength-training/)
- **[PRACTICE]** For runners (as opposed to alpinists), Uphill Athlete keeps general strength short because too much time on it "detracts from running volume". — [UA forum](https://uphillathlete.com/forums/topic/tftna-vs-tftua-strength-training/)
- **[EVIDENCE]** A meta-analysis of tapering in endurance athletes (PLOS ONE 2023): a taper of **≤21 days** that cuts **volume by 41–60%** while keeping **intensity and frequency** is effective, and **8–14 days** appears optimal. This applies to endurance training, not gym work. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0282838)
- **[EVIDENCE]** There is very little strength-specific taper evidence, even for strength athletes. A powerlifting review notes the shortage of peaking protocols. — [PMC7552788](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7552788/)
- **[PRACTICE]** Coaching recommendation: **stop heavy work 2–3 weeks before a marathon** and keep light maintenance until about 2 weeks out, because neuromuscular fatigue from strength training lasts several days. — [RunnersConnect](https://runnersconnect.net/when-should-i-stop-strength-training-at-the-gym-before-the-marathon/)
- **[EVIDENCE]** The repeated-bout effect from downhill running lasts up to about 6 weeks and fades over time, so at least one downhill session in the weeks before the race is recommended. — [PMC7606541](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7606541/)

### Inferences
- A possible scheme for a trail or ultra goal race (synthesis, **not validated as a whole**):
  - **General preparation (4–8 weeks)**: 2×/week, movement quality, unilateral work, calves, core, introductory plyometrics.
  - **Maximal strength (4–8 weeks)**: 2×/week, heavy (≥80–85% 1RM, 3–5 × 3–6), plyometrics before the lifts.
  - **Specific phase (6–8 weeks before)**: ME and power (weighted step-ups, plyo step-ups, eccentric work), 1–2×/week; increase hill and downhill running.
  - **In season / maintenance**: 1×/week, heavy, low volume.
  - **Taper**: drop new or highly eccentric stimuli about 10–21 days before; a short heavy session with few sets can stay until about 7–10 days out, and the last downhill or eccentric session should fall roughly 10–14 days out. This is a coaching inference based on DOMS lasting several days and on the repeated-bout effect still being present at 3 weeks.
- Avoid **new** eccentric work in the final weeks: exercises the athlete is already used to cause much less damage, thanks to the repeated-bout effect.

### Gaps
- No study was found that sets how many days before a trail or ultra race to stop heavy or eccentric strength work.
- No direct evidence on how strength-training periodization affects ultra performance (as opposed to running economy).

## 4. Sequencing strength and running on the same day (interference, recovery, "hard days hard")

### Takeaway
In trained endurance athletes, interference is small. When both are on the same day, the practical rule is to separate them by **≥3–6 h**. Strength first favours neuromuscular adaptations, while easy running beforehand causes little interference. Put strength and plyometrics on **hard running days** (after a quality session, or in the afternoon) rather than before them, and **avoid plyometrics after hard running**, which reduces their effectiveness and raises injury risk.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE]** A meta-analysis on concurrent training (Sports Medicine 2023, sex and training status): VO2max improvement was blunted in untrained people but **not in trained or highly trained endurance athletes**. Interference with lower-body strength and power was small to moderate in untrained people. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01943-9)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Frontiers 2025 review: training order has **no significant effect** on aerobic endurance, hypertrophy or maximal strength in most human studies, but **strength → endurance favours neuromuscular adaptations**. Low-intensity endurance training can come before strength without obvious interference. Recommended spacing is **≥3 h**. — [Frontiers fspor.2025.1692399](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1692399/full)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Separating sessions by **6 h** gave larger strength gains than doing both in the same session (study cited in a 2022 review). — [Hughes 2022 (Glos eprints)](https://eprints.glos.ac.uk/10611/9/10611-Hughes-(2022)-Concurrent-training.pdf); [DiVA – same vs split session](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1442413/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Doing strength first improved lower-body dynamic strength more in a 9-week study of moderately active men, with no differences in aerobic fitness. — [PLOS ONE 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0233134)
- **[EVIDENCE]** A review on the effect of training order on endurance performance found that the sequence does not change endurance outcomes. — [PMC11359207](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11359207/)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Interference depends on dose. High endurance volumes impair strength, power and hypertrophy more, and running interferes more than cycling or swimming. — [Hughes 2022](https://eprints.glos.ac.uk/10611/9/10611-Hughes-(2022)-Concurrent-training.pdf); [Rønnestad PDF](https://www.euskadi.eus/contenidos/informacion/kirol_eskola_prestakuntza_jard/eu_def/adjuntos/R%C3%B8nnestad.pdf)
- **[PRACTICE / expert]** Blagrove: fatigue from hard running sessions **impairs the development of explosive strength**, and plyometrics after hard running carry an **injury risk**. — [Blagrove slides 2025](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/57f8526ce3df285aab609e84/t/67cac1c36189bc149514b897/1741341125503/Strength+for+Endurance+-+Principles+of+S&C+Mar25+-+Rich+Blagrove.pdf)
- **[PRACTICE]** In Giovanelli's ultra study, as reported secondarily, sessions were **skipped the day after very long or intense runs**. — [Sports Performance Bulletin](https://sportsperformancebulletin.com/training/strength-conditioning--flexibility/distance-running-why-strength-matters)

### Inferences
- A typical "hard days hard" week: morning quality session (intervals or hills), strength in the afternoon or evening (≥3–6 h later), and an easy day or rest the next day. If strength must come first, run easy or after a gap. Keep strength and plyometrics away from the day before the long run or a key session. Strength 48 h before a key session is generally tolerated, but this is practice, not evidence.
- For ultra runners in a high-volume phase, a short heavy session (25–40 min) limits interference more than long circuits.

### Gaps
- The Doma et al. review on how strength training affects later running performance (24–48 h) was not found in the searches.
- No trail-specific data on how gym DOMS interacts with technical running or downhill sessions.

## 5. Youth and beginners vs advanced athletes; road vs trail vs ultra

### Takeaway
Beginners benefit from **maximal, high-force, low-velocity** work introduced gradually, with a priority on technique. Advanced athletes use more complex sequences (strength → power, specific ME). Youth training is safe and effective **with qualified supervision**, per the 2014 international consensus. Trail and ultra differ from road in the need for eccentric strength, single-leg work and calf strength, the higher injury rate, and local leg fatigue. For ultras, the trade-off between strength training and running volume becomes more important.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE/consensus]** Lloyd et al. 2014, *BJSM* 48:498–505 (international consensus, endorsed by the AAP and AMSSM among others): youth resistance training improves strength, power, **running speed**, change of direction and motor skills, **as long as** programmes are supervised by qualified professionals and suited to the child's needs and abilities. — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/7/498); [PDF](https://g-se.com/uploads/blog_adjuntos/br_j_sports_med_2014_lloyd_498_505.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Plyometrics combined with strength training gave larger running-economy gains in **younger** (ES 0.95) and **highly trained** runners (ES 0.94). — [Kinesiology 2023](https://ojs.srce.hr/kinesiology/article/view/24698)
- **[EVIDENCE/review]** Beginners should start with maximal, high-force, low-velocity work. Advanced athletes should follow a strength-endurance → basic strength → strength → power sequence. — [SCJ 2015 (ETSU)](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works/3781)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Interference is small or absent in trained and highly trained athletes, and larger in untrained ones. — [Sports Med 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01943-9)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Trail runners: injury rates of 10.7–19.6 per 1,000 h vs 2.5–5.8 for road. — [Frontiers Bioeng. 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2025.1690023/full). Trail and ultra: greater muscle damage (−41% knee-extensor strength after 156 km), and strength becomes a stronger predictor as distance increases (about 100 km). — [Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1734785/full)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Elite trail runners: after the 2023 World Championships, jump height and handgrip did not fall, but **reactive strength index** did. Shorter races stress reactive qualities. — [Frontiers fspor.2025.1506067](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1506067/full)
- **[PRACTICE]** Uphill Athlete: for runners, general strength stays short so it doesn't take from running volume. Climbers and alpinists, who move slowly under heavy loads, benefit more from it. — [UA forum](https://uphillathlete.com/forums/topic/tftna-vs-tftua-strength-training/)
- **[PRACTICE]** Roche coaches athletes "from pros to beginners" with the same short 10-minute routine, and personally avoids heavy lifts because of prolonged soreness. This is a minimalist approach that contrasts with the heavy-load literature. — [Trail Runner Mag](https://www.trailrunnermag.com/training/trail-tips-training/ultra-legs-a-strength-routine-for-ultrarunners/); [TTS444](https://scientifictriathlon.com/tts444/)
- **[PRACTICE]** Beginner and intermediate trail plans need gym equipment and include upper-body work for pole use. — [TrainingPeaks](https://trainingpeaks.com/training-plans/running/trail/tp-517315/strength-training-for-trailrunners)

### Inferences
- **Beginners and youth**: 2×/week, technique first with bodyweight or light loads, then progressive loading (moderate-to-heavy loads once technique is solid). Plyometrics at low volume with landing work. Supervision is essential for youth.
- **Advanced**: heavy loads (≥80–85% 1RM), more demanding plyometrics (depth jumps, bounding), specific ME before the race, and in-season maintenance at 1×/week.
- **Road vs trail vs ultra**: road puts more weight on reactive and plyometric work and running economy at speed. Trail adds eccentric and downhill work, unilateral strength and ankle stability. Ultra adds muscular endurance and fatigue resistance, protection against cumulative eccentric damage, and lower total gym volume to protect running volume.
- Coaches disagree (heavy loads in the literature and Uphill Athlete vs minimalism in Roche). This is a point of debate to make explicit in a final report.

### Gaps
- The 2014 consensus's sets, reps and frequency for youth, and its specific guidance for young runners, were not available in the extracts.
- No RCT was found comparing strength protocols across road, trail and ultra runners. The differences are inferred from physiological demands and practice.
- Trail Running Movement content and interviews with elite trail coaches on gym programmes were not found.
