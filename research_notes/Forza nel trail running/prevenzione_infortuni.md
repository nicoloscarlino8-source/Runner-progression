# Strength training for injury prevention and durability in trail and road running

Scope: strength, balance and eccentric training for injury risk reduction, tissue resilience and fatigue resistance ("durability") in trail and distance runners. Evidence strength is flagged throughout: **[High]** = meta-analysis with consistent RCTs, **[Moderate]** = single good RCT or a meta-analysis with caveats, **[Low]** = small, low-quality or extrapolated, **[Very low/expert]** = coaching or mechanistic only.

---

## 1. Meta-analyses on strength training and injury reduction (sport in general vs runners specifically)

### Takeaway
Across sports in general, strength training is the strongest exercise-based injury preventive. Lauersen 2014 and 2018 both put risk at about one-third of control (RR ≈ 0.32–0.34). Runner-specific evidence is weaker. The best meta-analysis of endurance runners (Wu 2024) found no significant overall effect, although supervised programmes did reduce risk in a post hoc analysis. The largest runner RCT of an unsupervised 10-min home programme was null (Toresdahl 2020). A foot-core RCT was positive (Taddei 2020).

### Cited Findings
- **Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen 2014 (BJSM 48:871-877)** pooled 25 trials with 26,610 participants and 3,464 injuries across all sports. For the strength-training subgroup, RR = 0.315 (95% CI 0.207–0.480), i.e. risk fell to under one-third of control. Overuse injuries "could be almost halved". Stretching showed no benefit (RR 0.963). The subgroup rests on few trials and overall heterogeneity was high. **[Moderate–High]** — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/11/871); [BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/2014/07/20/exercises-to-prevent-sports-injuries-lots-of-talk-but-do-they-work/)
- **Lauersen, Andersen & Andersen 2018 (BJSM 52(24):1557-1563)** pooled 6 RCTs with 7,738 participants aged 12–40 and 177 acute or overuse injuries. The cluster-adjusted ITT RR was 0.338 (95% CI 0.238–0.480), and the authors rated the strength of evidence as "high". They also reported a dose-response: a 10% increase in strength-training volume cut injury risk by more than 4 percentage points (an absolute, not relative, reduction). Participants were not runners specifically (mostly team and youth sports). — [BJSM article info](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/24/1557.info); [NIH Brage record](https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/handle/11250/2579164?show=full)
- **Wu, Brooke-Wavell, Fong, Paquette & Blagrove 2024 (Sports Med 54(5):1249-1267)** pooled 9 studies with 1,904 endurance runners. Programmes included lunges and squats, plyometrics, core work and foot strengthening. Overall there was no significant difference in injury risk (z = −1.60, p = 0.110) or injury rate (z = −0.98, p = 0.329). A post hoc analysis of **supervised** interventions found significantly lower injury risk (z = −3.75, p < 0.001). Seven of the 9 studies were rated low quality. The authors suggest supervision, possibly through better compliance, may be essential. **[Low–Moderate]** — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11127851/); [Springer DOI](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-024-01993-7)
- **Toresdahl et al. 2020 (Sports Health)** randomised 720 first-time NYC Marathon runners, 12 weeks out, to an unsupervised 10-min programme 3×/week (quadriceps, hip abductors, core, delivered by written and video instruction) or to observation. Overuse injury that prevented the marathon occurred in 7.1% vs 7.3%, with no difference in finishing time. **[Moderate, null]** — [Sports Health](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1941738119877180); [Healio summary](https://www.healio.com/orthopedics/sports-medicine/news/online/%7Be8f9a2a6-cabe-44a9-90ae-ab25320af370%7D/number-of-weekly-training-runs-may-increase-injury-risk-in-first-time-marathon-runners)
- **Taddei et al. 2020 (AJSM)** was a single-blind RCT of 118 recreational runners. An 8-week foot-core programme (12 foot-ankle intrinsic and extrinsic exercises) was compared with 5-min placebo stretching, with 12-month follow-up. It reduced running-related injury risk. A secondary review reports about 2.4× lower injury incidence (this number was not verified from the primary abstract). PEDro 8/10. Generalisability to novices and elites is untested. **[Moderate]** — [ACFAS review](https://www.acfas.org/slr-october-2021-kevan-patel); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/62997); [Physio-Network](https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/ankle-foot/foot-core-training-to-prevent-running-related-injuries-a-survival-analysis-of-a-single-blind-randomized-controlled-trial/)
- A narrative review notes that muscle weakness (especially of the hip) characterises injured runners, but evidence that weakness *causes* running injury is lacking. — [LIDA record: strength training, distance running performance and injury prevention](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4092479)
- A 2026 narrative review concludes that current evidence "does not consistently demonstrate a significant reduction in injury incidence" after strength interventions in runners. — [cited via Toresdahl search results; MDPI / apcz review](https://apcz.umk.pl/QS/article/view/72956)
- Observational BJSM work from Toresdahl's group on 2019 NYC Marathon runners linked rapid training increases (load errors) with injury, more than lack of strength. — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/going-the-distance-taking-a-closer-look-at-the-injury-risks-for-first-time-marathon-runners)

### Inferences
- The "strength training cuts injuries by two-thirds" headline is generalised from mixed-sport, mostly team and youth populations. It should not be presented as established for runners.
- In runners, injuries are dominated by load-management errors (overuse). Strength training is likely a modifier that helps when it is adequately dosed, progressive and complied with (supervised or heavy enough), rather than a stand-alone preventive. Low-dose, unsupervised "prehab" circuits (Toresdahl) appear insufficient.
- Targeted, tissue-specific programmes (foot core, ankle balance after sprain, calf loading) currently have better runner-specific support than generic programmes.
- Dose matters (Lauersen 2018 dose-response; Wu 2024 supervision effect). This supports 2+ sessions per week of progressive, adequately heavy work.

### Gaps
- No RCT of strength training for injury prevention specifically in **trail** runners was found.
- The full Lauersen 2018 dose-response confidence intervals and baseline risk could not be retrieved, so the relative-risk equivalent of the "4 percentage points" figure is unknown.
- I could not retrieve the 2024 umbrella review of running-injury risk factors (Correia et al., J Sport Health Sci).

## 2. Common trail-running injuries and targeted strength, balance and proprioception interventions

### Takeaway
Trail injury epidemiology is heterogeneous. In Viljoen 2021 the foot was the most common site, followed by knee, lower leg, thigh and ankle. One 2024 review found ankle injuries (49.5%) and tendinopathies (29.8%) predominated in trail runners. Interventions with the best evidence:
- Proprioceptive and balance training cuts recurrent ankle sprains by about 35% [High].
- Heavy slow resistance (HSR) and Alfredson eccentric loading are equally effective treatments for midportion Achilles tendinopathy, and HSR has better adherence [Moderate].
- Hip plus knee strengthening treats patellofemoral pain [Moderate–High, treatment].
- High-load heel raises give faster short-term improvement in plantar fasciitis [Low–Moderate].
- Foot-core training lowers running injury risk [Moderate].
Most tendon, PFP and plantar evidence is for *treatment*, not primary prevention.

### Cited Findings
**Epidemiology**
- **Viljoen et al. 2021 (Sports Med)** pooled 16 studies with 8,644 trail runners, mostly race-based. Incidence ranged from 1.6 to 4,285 injuries per 1000 h, reflecting extreme heterogeneity. By site: foot > knee > lower leg > thigh > ankle. Common diagnoses were skin lacerations and abrasions, blisters, muscle strains and ligament sprains. Uneven surfaces raise the risk of ankle sprains and falls. — [UP repository](https://repository.up.ac.za/handle/2263/80943); [PDF](https://repository.up.ac.za/bitstream/2263/80943/1/Viljoen_Epidemiology_2021a.pdf)
- A **2024 systematic review in Retos** (19 studies, comparing trail, cross-country and orienteering runners) found trail runners had the highest share of ankle injuries (49.5%) by location and of tendinopathies (29.8%) by diagnosis. This conflicts with Viljoen on the leading site. — [Retos / DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/62113e5634b048b49ebcb14a53d31281); [Retos](https://recyt.fecyt.es/index.php/retos/article/view/99532)
- A **living systematic review** of trail-running injury risk factors (Univ. Pretoria group) is updated periodically. — [UP repository](https://repository.up.ac.za/handle/2263/90063?show=full)
- Plantar fasciitis is reported to account for up to about 10% of running injuries. This comes from a clinical blog. **[Low]** — [PainScience](https://www.painscience.com/biblio/strengthening-helps-plantar-fasciitis.html)

**Ankle sprain: balance and proprioceptive training**
- **Schiftan, Ross & Hahne 2015 (J Sci Med Sport)**: meta-analysis of 7 RCTs, 3,726 sporting participants.
  - All participants: RR 0.65 (0.55–0.77).
  - Participants with a prior sprain: RR 0.64 (0.51–0.81), NNT ≈ 13.
  - Primary prevention: RR 0.57 (0.34–0.97), but pooled from two non-significant trials, so the evidence is "inconclusive".
  - The search ran to Oct 2013. **[High for recurrence]** — [PubMed 24831756](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24831756/); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/45503)
- Bracing and neuromuscular/proprioceptive training are both described as effective against recurrent sprains in athletes. — [World Physio abstract](https://world.physio/congress-proceeding/effectiveness-proprioceptive-and-neuromuscular-training-compared-bracing); [NATA J Athl Train](https://nata.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/attr/52/11/article-p1065.xml)

**Achilles tendinopathy: HSR vs Alfredson eccentric**
- **Beyer et al. 2015 (AJSM)** randomised 58 patients with chronic midportion Achilles tendinopathy to Alfredson eccentric training or HSR for 12 weeks, with 52-week follow-up.
  - Both groups improved in function (VISA-A), pain during activity and tendon structure, with no between-group differences, and gains were maintained at 52 weeks.
  - Satisfaction at 12 weeks was 100% with HSR vs 80% with eccentric (p = 0.052).
  - Session compliance was 92% with HSR vs 78% with eccentric (significant).
  - PEDro 5/10, unblinded. **[Moderate]** — [ClinicalTrials NCT00952042](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00952042); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/43703); [Full text PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Heavy%20Slow%20Resistance%20Versus%20Eccentric%20Training%20as%20Treatment%20for%20Achilles%20Tendinopathy%20-%20A%20Randomized%20Controlled%20Trial.pdf); [MyOrthoEvidence](https://myorthoevidence.com/AceReport/Show/heavy-slow-resistance-and-eccentric-training-comparable-in-achilles-tendinopathy)

**Patellofemoral pain (PFP) / ITB**
- **5th International Patellofemoral Pain Research Retreat consensus (BJSM 2018; 52(18):1170-1178)** covers exercise therapy and physical interventions for PFP. I could not access the specific recommendations. — cited in [Brighton MSK blog](https://blogs.brighton.ac.uk/musculoskeletalphysiotherapy/2019/07/08/the-outcome-of-hip-exercise-in-patellofemoral-pain-a-systematic-review/)
- On hip vs knee strengthening, one systematic review found the two "equally effective", with limited evidence that adding hip exercise helps. Another review (14 studies) found hip-inclusive programmes beat quadriceps-only programmes for pain and function. Programmes predominantly target the hip abductors and external rotators. — [Brighton MSK blog](https://blogs.brighton.ac.uk/musculoskeletalphysiotherapy/2019/07/08/the-outcome-of-hip-exercise-in-patellofemoral-pain-a-systematic-review/); [HCPLive](https://hcplive.com/view/hip_exercises); [RSD Journal review](https://rsdjournal.org/rsd/article/view/44261)

**Plantar fasciitis**
- **Rathleff et al. 2015 (Scand J Med Sci Sports)** randomised 48 patients with ultrasound-verified plantar fasciitis to inserts plus stretching or to inserts plus high-load unilateral heel raises with a towel under the toes. The heel raises used 3 s concentric, 2 s isometric pause and 3 s eccentric, every second day, progressing from 12RM to 8RM.
  - At 3 months, the Foot Function Index was 29 points lower in the strength group.
  - There was no difference from 6 months on (FFI at 12 months: 22 vs 16).
  - Participants were general patients, not runners. PEDro 6/10. **[Low–Moderate]** — [BJSM PDF](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2015/01/23/bjsports-2014-094562.full.pdf); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/42802); [BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/2014/09/15/plantar-fasciitis-important-new-research-by-michael-rathleff/)

**Foot-core (general running injury prevention)**
- See Taddei 2020 in Section 1. — [ACFAS review](https://www.acfas.org/slr-october-2021-kevan-patel)

### Inferences
- For trail runners, the highest-value targeted prevention tools are:
  - ankle balance and proprioceptive training, especially for anyone with a prior sprain (secondary prevention is well supported);
  - progressive calf and Achilles loading (HSR-style heel raises, likely seated and standing);
  - foot-core work;
  - hip abductor, extensor and quadriceps strengthening for knee complaints.
- HSR is a pragmatic choice over Alfredson's daily eccentric protocol (2×/day for 12 weeks) because outcomes are equal and adherence is better.
- Ankle exposure on technical terrain plausibly makes balance training more relevant to trail than to road runners. This is an inference; no trail-specific trials were found.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests strength or balance training for *primary* prevention of Achilles tendinopathy, PFP, ITBS or plantar fasciitis in runners.
- No ITBS-specific hip abductor trial evidence was retrieved.
- Details of the original Alfredson 1998 protocol (3×15 eccentric heel drops, bent and straight knee, 2×/day, 12 weeks) were not verified in this session, beyond its role as the comparator in Beyer 2015.
- No newer (post-2015) ankle-sprain meta-analysis was retrieved.
- Bone stress injury (BSI) is covered in Section 5.

## 3. Eccentric strength, downhill running, DOMS and the repeated bout effect

### Takeaway
Downhill running causes large eccentric muscle damage and strength loss in knee extensors and plantar flexors, similar to the losses after an ultra-trail race. A single or a few prior downhill or eccentric bouts produce a well-documented repeated bout effect (RBE) in untrained and recreational runners. Whether regular downhill intervals add protection in *experienced* trail runners is unresolved. Low-load eccentric and isometric preconditioning also protects. Protection appears mode-specific and wanes over weeks.

### Cited Findings
- Prior downhill running (DR) reduces muscle-damage markers and strength loss (RBE) in untrained people and recreational runners. High-intensity DR produces knee extensor and plantar flexor strength losses similar to an ultra-trail race. No studies have examined whether regular DR intervals add protection in experienced trail runners. — [PMC12846201](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12846201); [Penyagolosa Trails research PDF](https://www.penyagolosatrails.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/21-Downhill-Running-Induced-Muscle-Damage-in-Trail-Runners.pdf)
- In a downhill training study, muscle pain and plasma CK rose after the first 40-min downhill run and did not rise further over subsequent training, which is consistent with an RBE. — [SciELO, Anais Acad Bras Ciênc 2022](https://scielo.br/j/aabc/a/BqpmvX7m9xNXkrDbRpKp6Fs/?lang=en)
- Low-load eccentric exercise protects against damage from later intense eccentric exercise. That protection lasts 2 weeks but not 3 weeks. — [PMC1332286 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1332286/); [SciELO 2022](https://scielo.br/j/aabc/a/BqpmvX7m9xNXkrDbRpKp6Fs/?lang=en)
- Isometric preconditioning: 10 maximal isometric leg-press contractions at long muscle length, performed 2–4 days before a 30-min −15% downhill treadmill run, reduced muscle damage in 30 untrained men. — [Wintec research archive](https://researcharchive.wintec.ac.nz/id/eprint/132/)
- Specificity: in a US Marine Corps study, prior downhill running did **not** protect against soreness, CK or strength loss from unaccustomed loaded mountain hiking. Changes in mode and external load may limit cross-transfer. — [DTIC ADA326083](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA326083.pdf)
- Coaching guidance (not trial-based): most adaptation comes after a handful of moderate downhill runs, maintained by exposure every 2–3 weeks. One suggested way is moderately hard downhills inside the weekly long run. The proposed mechanisms are neural (motor-unit recruitment) and connective-tissue changes. Big downhill sessions carry high impact and injury risk. **[Very low/expert]** — [TrainRight](https://trainright.com/downhill-running-protect-against-muscle-damage/); [Trail Runner Mag](https://www.trailrunnermag.com/training/trail-tips/last-longer-downhills-training-eccentric-muscle-contractions?scope=anon)

### Inferences
- For trail and ultra preparation, include specific downhill running exposures that progress in grade and volume and are repeated at least every 2–3 weeks to maintain the RBE. Add gym eccentric and heavy work for quads and calves (e.g. slow-eccentric squats, split squats, step-downs, heel raises). Gym work alone is unlikely to fully substitute for terrain-specific downhill exposure, given the mode-specificity findings.
- Schedule the first downhill block well before (≥3–6 weeks) the target race and avoid novel eccentric loads in the final 2 weeks. This is an inference from RBE time-course data.

### Gaps
- No human RCT was found testing heavy gym-based eccentric resistance training as preconditioning against downhill-running damage or ultra-race strength loss.
- Optimal downhill grade, volume and frequency for experienced trail runners is not established. The Penyagolosa-linked study addresses this, but its results were not verified.

## 4. Durability / physiological resilience and the role of strength training

### Takeaway
Durability (Maunder et al. 2021) is the time of onset and magnitude of deterioration in physiological-profiling characteristics (VO2max, thresholds, economy) during prolonged exercise. Jones & Kirby 2025 reframed it as "physiological resilience", a fourth determinant alongside VO2max, economy and thresholds. Heavy strength and plyometric training appear to improve resilience, but the data are scant and mainly from cycling. Endurance training alone has not been shown to improve durability in well-trained athletes.

### Cited Findings
- **Definition**: Maunder et al. 2021 define durability as the "time of onset and magnitude of deterioration in physiological-profiling characteristics during prolonged exercise". Hunter, Maunder et al. (2025 methods review) describe it as resilience to deterioration of physiological variables and performance during or after prolonged exercise. It is measured via HR drift, threshold or economy drift, or a maximal performance test after prolonged work. — [AUT repository: Durability methodological considerations](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/e4ed557d-8c56-44ba-9eac-520fc0ae1aae/full); [London Met repository](https://repository.londonmet.ac.uk/10267/); [Herts UHRA](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/25575/)
- **Jones & Kirby 2025, "Physiological Resilience: What Is It and How Might It Be Trained?" (Scand J Med Sci Sports 35:e70032)**:
  - Resilience is added to a modified Joyner model. Start-line VO2max, economy and thresholds deteriorate during prolonged exercise, with large individual variability.
  - Resistance training, especially heavy strength and plyometric training, appears to have positive effects on resilience.
  - Other candidate strategies are years of consistent training and regular prolonged sessions with intense or progressive efforts.
  - These strategies are framed as hypotheses with preliminary data. — [PMC11872681](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11872681/); [PDF](https://fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Physiological-Resilience.pdf)
- Endurance training alone has not increased durability in well-trained athletes (citing Hausswirth 2010 and Rønnestad 2011). Rønnestad's work showed concurrent heavy strength training preserved late-race power output in cyclists. The most effective methods for training durability are unknown. — [Frontiers Physiol 2023, 14:1128111](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1128111/pdf); [JYX copy](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/123456789/86261/1/fphys-14-1128111.pdf)
- **Malinen et al. 2026, "Determinants of Physiological Resilience Among Recreational-To-Competitive Endurance Runners" (Scand J Med Sci Sports 36(7):e70349)** is a runner-specific study. I saw it only via secondary coverage, so its findings are unverified. — [Vitónica summary](https://www.vitonica.com/running-1/vo2max-economia-carrera-resiliencia-fisiologica-secreto-que-determina-tus-marcas-carrera-bicicleta)

### Inferences
- For trail and ultra runners, durability likely has a *mechanical/neuromuscular* component that is plausibly the target of strength work: eccentric damage resistance, maintained economy late in races, and preserved knee-extensor and plantar-flexor force. This links Section 3 (RBE) with Section 4.
- In practice, heavy strength (≥80% 1RM-type loading) plus plyometrics 2×/week, combined with long runs that include late-session quality, aligns with current hypotheses. Evidence remains **[Low]**.

### Gaps
- No RCT in trail or ultra runners tests strength training against durability outcomes (e.g. economy drift after 2–4 h, or downhill-induced strength loss).
- The Maunder 2021 original (Sports Med) and the Hausswirth and Rønnestad primary data were not retrieved directly. Their statements come via secondary reviews.

## 5. Female runners, masters runners and bone health

### Takeaway
Bone stress injury (BSI) risk in runners, particularly women and off-road runners, is driven primarily by low energy availability (REDs, IOC 2023) and load errors. Strength training's role in BSI prevention is plausible but not directly trialled. Heavy resistance plus impact training raises BMD in postmenopausal women (LIFTMOR: +2.9% lumbar spine in 8 months), which is relevant to female and masters runners. Masters runners maintain BMD over 4–5 years, but power-trained masters athletes have better hip BMD than endurance athletes. This supports adding heavy and power work.

### Cited Findings
- The 2023 **IOC REDs consensus** is the reference statement. Low energy availability impairs bone health and raises BSI risk, and it commonly affects runners. — [AAFP 2024 BSI review](https://aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2024/1200/bone-stress-injuries.html); [AUT document](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/c6fc132c-feaa-4e20-9a3a-11dd1832986c/content)
- **International Delphi consensus on BSI** (Hoenig, Kraus, Tenforde et al., BJSM): 41 experts across 3 rounds agreed on 41 of 58 statements on diagnosis, management, bone health and return to sport. Its strength-training statements were not retrieved. — [Stanford FASTR](https://med.stanford.edu/pedsortho/education/female-athlete-triad.html)
- **Stennett et al. 2025 (J Sport Rehabil)** is a systematic review of BSI epidemiology and risk factors in female off-road runners. Findings were not retrieved. — [OUCI record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/lRr1RxAM/)
- **LIFTMOR**: twice-weekly supervised heavy resistance plus impact training in postmenopausal women with osteopenia or osteoporosis produced +2.9% lumbar spine BMD over 8 months vs −1.2% in controls. Femoral neck BMD and functional strength also improved. Participants were not runners. **[Moderate]** — [Relias Media](https://reliasmedia.com/articles/28597-running-calcium-and-resistance-training-slow-bone-mineral-density-loss-in-females)
- **Masters runners**:
  - A 5-year study of women masters runners found age was the only significant predictor of bone change, and running did not reduce BMD.
  - Male masters runners maintained hip and spine BMD over 4–5 years.
  - Optimising bone health and preventing BSI are priorities for masters runners, but BSI risk in this group is poorly characterised. — [Nutrition-Evidence: Bone health and the masters runner](https://nutrition-evidence.com/article/496055/bone-health-and-the-masters-runner); [Gerontology J](https://biomed.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/57/4/M203)
- Masters sprinters have greater hip BMD T-scores, lean mass and lower-limb power than age-matched masters endurance athletes. This suggests power training benefits bone. — [Physiological Society](https://www.physoc.org/?p=96296); [Georgia Southern: masters weightlifters vs runners](https://scholars.georgiasouthern.edu/en/publications/bone-mineral-density-and-muscle-mass-in-masters-olympic-weightlif/fingerprints/)
- A claim that strength training preserves tendon stiffness in masters athletes appeared only in a commercial blog. **[Unverified]** — [Sundried blog](https://www.sundried.com/blogs/guide/strength-training-programming-for-master-endurance-athletes-bone-density-and-power)

### Inferences
- Female, masters and perimenopausal or postmenopausal runners should prioritise heavy resistance (≈80–85% 1RM, low reps) plus impact or plyometric loading, because running alone is a site-specific and incomplete osteogenic stimulus (e.g. for the spine and hip). Energy availability must be addressed first, since no strength programme compensates for REDs.
- Masters runners may gain more from strength and power work than younger runners, both for bone and for the age-related declines in muscle power that likely affect downhill eccentric tolerance. This is an inference; direct data are thin.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests resistance training for BSI prevention in runners.
- No sex-specific data on strength-training injury-prevention efficacy in runners were found.
- No peer-reviewed data on tendon properties and strength training in masters runners were retrieved.
- The menstrual-cycle and menopause-specific injury-prevention literature was not covered.

### Practical protocol synthesis (for the report writer; derived from the sources above)
- **Frequency and dose**: at least 2 sessions/week, progressive, ideally supervised or coached. Supervision and volume appear to matter (Wu 2024; Lauersen 2018).
- **Calf and Achilles**: HSR-style heel raises 3×/week, 3–4 s per phase, progressing load as in Beyer 2015 (e.g. 15RM→6RM over 12 weeks), seated and standing. For plantar fascia, the Rathleff variant is single-leg heel raises with a towel under the toes, 3 s up, 2 s hold, 3 s down, 12RM→8RM every other day.
- **Foot core**: 8-week intrinsic and extrinsic foot programme (Taddei 2020).
- **Ankle**: single-leg balance and wobble-board progressions, especially for anyone with a prior sprain (≈35% recurrence reduction).
- **Hip and knee**: hip abductor and external rotator plus quadriceps strengthening.
- **Eccentric and downhill**: progressive downhill running exposure repeated at least every 2–3 weeks, plus slow-eccentric quad work. Avoid novel eccentric loading within about 2 weeks of a race.
- **Durability and bone**: heavy strength plus plyometrics, especially for female and masters runners. Ensure adequate energy availability.
